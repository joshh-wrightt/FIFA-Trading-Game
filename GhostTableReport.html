📰 The world that never was · what trading actually did

The tournament is over and Divisha is champion. Strip every trade out of the ledger — hand everyone back their original four teams and their opening bank — and a different manager tops the table: Tim. But read that carefully, because the ghost table is easy to misread. It is not a scoreboard of what people threw away. It's a frozen parallel universe in which nobody trades — and the gap between it and reality is mostly the story of everyone else's dealing, not your own.

📈 First — how the asset actually works

A team is a depreciating, dividend-paying asset. Every time it plays it pays out its points — win 2, draw 1 — to whoever holds it at that moment, and then it is worth a little less, because it has one fewer match left to pay. Those banked points are locked forever: once they're yours, no trade can claw them back. That one rule reframes the whole table. Selling a team is never selling the points it has already paid you — it's selling its remaining dividends for cash today. Do it when the cash beats what's left to come and you win the trade; the only way trading costs you real points is selling a share for less than it goes on to pay.

📉 Where selling genuinely cost points · Josh & AJ

Here is the flip side the dividend rule warns about: sell a share for less than it goes on to pay, and you really do leak points. Josh was also drafted Spain — a 22-point ghost hand — and sold it early; his real points fell to just 9, and he finished 22nd. AJ did the same with a strong England-and-Egypt hand: 24 ghost points sold down to 10 real, adrift in 21st. These are the trades that actually hurt — and note what the damage was: not the act of selling, but the price. Both let dividend-rich teams go too cheap, for less than the points those teams still had left to pay.

🔁 The serial dealer · Michael

Michael, across every edition of this report, keeps churning a good hand into a worse one: 23 ghost points down to 18 real, a frozen 3rd turned into a real 14th. The most active trader in the office and, by the points, the one who most often sold dividends for less than they went on to pay. Activity isn't the edge; pricing is.

🧮 The verdict · your points are safe, your rank is not

The subtlety of the ghost table is this: because banked points can never be traded back, your own good trades rarely cost you — Tim, Divisha and Rohit all finished ahead of their frozen selves. What drops you down this table is mostly everyone else dealing well around you. The standings are relative: a rival harvesting dividends better than you pushes your rank down without touching a single one of your points. The real exceptions are the genuine mispricings — Josh's and AJ's cut-price Spain and England — where a share was sold for less than its remaining dividends and points actually bled away. 14 managers ended up higher than their frozen rank, 9 lower. The draw hands you a portfolio of depreciating assets; the game is knowing what each still has left to pay, and getting the price right.
