♔ The King is dead

Look at the top of the table and nothing has changed: Paulius still sits first on 23.39, a clear 1.54 above the field. Look again — closely — and you are staring at a death mask. Of the eight team-shares in his hand, exactly one club still has a pulse (USA ×2). The other six — Czechia ×2, Uzbekistan ×2, Jordan ×2 — are eliminated. His total is no longer a lead. It is a frozen number, a monument to points already banked, with almost no machine left to make more. He is the king of a country that has stopped producing.

The model is blunt about it. Run the remaining bracket two hundred thousand times and Paulius converts his table-topping lead into a title only 8.5% of the time — sixth on the win-odds board, behind people he is nominally beating by ten points. A leader who is a 12-to-1 outsider to actually win is not a leader. He is a placeholder.

🗡️ The demons he built with his own hands

Here is the cruel symmetry. Paulius's whole creed was the win–win trade — the deal where both parties walk away smiling, where he skims a little edge and the counterparty gets a team they love. Noble. Sociable. Fatal. Because every counterparty he made happy is now a loaded gun pointed back at him, and the names at the sharp end of the title race are, almost to a man, his own creations:

  • Tim — the new face at the top of the living table — is carrying Morocco ×2. One of those Moroccos was sold to him by Paulius on the very first afternoon (5 June, for 2.15). That Morocco just dumped the Netherlands out of the Round of 32 and sits in the last 16. Paulius built the asset that now leads the chase.
  • Echo, the man whose climb defies the spreadsheet, holds Canada ×2 — one of them handed over by Paulius (17 June) — already through to the Round of 16.
  • Divisha's "fortress," the double-Mexico the whole field now fears, was bought directly from Paulius (16 June, 5.77). He sold her the bricks and she built the wall.

He fought no monsters. He manufactured them, gift-wrapped them, took a small profit, and waved them out the door. Now they gaze back at him from the abyss of the knockout bracket. The abyss, as promised, gazes back.

👑 …long live the King

The crown does not sit empty for long. On the living board — strip out the frozen men and rank by who still has teams that can score points — the heir apparent is Tim (21.85, second overall but first among managers still in the fight): a strong, banked book plus Morocco in the last 16 and Argentina still standing. He is the new front-of-house King, and he got there, fittingly, off the old King's own trade.

But "long live the King" is a coronation, not a guarantee — because the win-odds model says this is no longer a one-man reign. It is a scrum. The heaviest title artillery belongs to the patient double-stackers Paulius himself names in his exit interview below: Neil's pocket-aces England ×2 (model favourite, 20.6%), Kerrill's Colombia ×2 + a fat bank (15.9%), Divisha's Mexico-and-Spain fortress (14.1%) and Jing's Switzerland ×2 + Ivory Coast ×2 (13.3%). The King is dead. Long live the dozen pretenders.

🪦 Michael Brown — Back From the Dead

Everybody had buried Michael. The low-EV bonfire — Senegal, double-Saudi, Scotland, Cape Verde — the negative bank, the dead-money reputation. The eulogies were written. Then Germany happened.

His Paraguay ×2 walked into the Round of 32 as 41st-ranked lottery tickets against a top-ten machine — and walked out winners on penalties, in what Al Jazeera called "potentially the greatest upset in World Cup history." Kai Havertz, ashen: "I'm lost for words… we've blown it twice." For Michael it was the opposite of words — it was two shares marching into the last 16 when his tournament was supposed to be over. The man threw the ball up with his eyes shut and watched it drop straight through the net.

Be clear-eyed: he is still hanging by a thread. The model gives him a fraction of a percent; one Cape Verde share and a double-Paraguay is not a campaign. But it is a pulse, and a thread is a thread — it holds right up until it doesn't. The door everyone slammed on him is now ajar. Underestimate the dead man at your peril; he has already done the one thing nobody priced.

🎙️ Two minutes with the resurrected man

So Michael, there has been a lot of talk recently about how you destroyed your chances in these past couple of weeks. What would you say to them now?

A lot of people say a lot of things half way through the game, but our focus is always the end result — and that's what we are gonna deliver.

I think when you see the trading that other people have done, it was essential to make moves and take advantage of situations — to both optimize your outcomes and also defend yourself from being disadvantaged. And ultimately, ultimately, the end result will be what matters, and we will see where we end up.

🎙️ The exit interview · Paulius, the deposed

We sat the former leader down while his lead was still technically intact and his hopes were not. What followed was equal parts trading-desk post-mortem, patriotic grievance, and scouting report on the men who took his throne. Transcript, lightly edited.

Four of your names went down — Uzbekistan, Jordan, Czechia, and the USA. Consecutive losses. Talk us through that stretch.

It was like watching four positions get margin-called on the same morning. Uzbekistan and Jordan were always lottery tickets, fine, I'd priced that. But the USA was a core holding. Czechia too. When your blue-chips and your moonshots blow up in the same window, that's not bad luck, that's correlation you didn't model.

Tim is now the clear frontrunner. Is the title gone?

Tim has the better book right now — his Sales derby win over Netherlands was huge for his chances. But "frontrunner" with a third of the tournament left is not "champion." If Tim wants this, he has to actually hold his lead through the bracket. Leads are easier to take than to keep. I'd know — I just demonstrated it.

That win came at your expense, though. Does it sting more losing the lead to Tim specifically?

Here's the part that actually hurts. Tim's American. I'm the one holding the USA — I bought the flag, I'm flying it, I'm the patriot in this building. And when the USA went down against Turkey, he's the one who came out ahead. The American sat back and let the Lithuanian carry the Stars and Stripes straight into a loss that paid him. There's no justice in that. I did the believing; he banked the dividend.

Out to Turkey, of all teams. Did you see that one coming?

Nobody had Turkey on the card. That's the cruelty of it — it wasn't Brazil, it wasn't France, it wasn't a result you could shrug off as "well, they ran into a giant." It was Turkey. I held the Stars and Stripes against Turkey and lost, and somewhere across the floor the actual American didn't lose a wink of sleep because for him it was just points going green. I took the patriotic risk. He collected the patriotic reward. Same flag, opposite outcomes.

So you're saying he should've been the one holding USA?

By every law of decency, yes. Instead I get the heartbreak and he gets the points. I'm out here more invested in his national team than he is. I should be granted green card for this.

Beyond your own table, what do you make of how the rest of the field is faring?

Mixed carnage. Michael's the one I can't look away from — he got a hail-mary out of Germany that had no business landing. That's a man who threw the ball up with his eyes closed and watched it go in. Good for him. But let's be honest about what he is: he's hanging by a thread. One result keeping him alive doesn't make a campaign. He's not playing the bracket, he's surviving it one heartbeat at a time, and a thread is a thread — it holds right up until it doesn't.

And Matt? He's gone entirely.

Matt got deleted. Twenty-four hours. Germany, Japan, and South Africa all eliminated inside a single day, and his whole book went with them. That's not a drawdown, that's a margin call that wiped the account before he could even pick up the phone. At least I'm bleeding out slowly — I get to suffer in stages, do the math, feel each one. Matt didn't even get the courtesy of a long goodbye. One bad day on the calendar and the tournament closed his position for him.

Forget Tim for a second. Who else scares you? Who's the one you're watching over your shoulder?

Tim's the leader, but he's not the only loaded gun at this table. Divisha is holding a monster — double Mexico and Spain. That's not a hand, that's a fortress. If those names run, she doesn't need anything to break her way; she just collects. Then Neil's — the man's sitting on double England, pocket aces. England gets a soft bracket and he's printing money while the rest of us scramble. And don't sleep on Kerrill: 2× Colombia plus a fat stack of cash, plus him calling the top on Uruguay. A loaded hand + smart trading is scary.

So it's the big stacks you fear.

Mostly — but that's the rookie mistake, only watching the leaders. I won't discount the underdogs. Jing and Haydee are both sitting on hands nobody's pricing properly. And then there's Echo, whose climb up the table makes no probabilistic sense, which is precisely why I respect it. You can model the favourites; you cannot model the guy who just keeps catching the result he needs. I'm not betting on Echo — but I'm absolutely watching him.

Looking ahead — which upcoming fixtures are you circling on the calendar?

There's one I genuinely can't take my eyes off: Echo versus Tim. The US Office derby — Canada against Morocco. And let me correct any impression that I'm neutral here, because I am desperately not. I need Echo's Canada to win. Canada beating Morocco is how Tim gets stopped — it's the single result that caps the runaway leader before he disappears over the horizon. So no popcorn, no theatre. I'm a Canada man now, fully, shamelessly. Go Echo. Bury him.

A strange position — rooting for a rival to take down the leader.

That's tail-player life. I can't catch Tim by being good; I can only catch him if someone else knocks him down. Echo's Canada is my proxy army. I don't gain a point from that match directly — but every dent in Tim's lead is oxygen for my fifteen percent. The enemy of my enemy is a Canadian. I've made peace with it.

And the fixture that scares you most?

Bosnia. May's holding double Bosnia, and this isn't about her climbing the table — she might not move much at all. It's about me. If Bosnia wins, I'm basically eliminated. Done. That's the result that doesn't bruise my book, it ends it. Everyone's watching the top of the table; I'm watching Bosnia, because that's the single fixture standing between me and a clean exit. My whole remaining fifteen percent is riding on Bosnia not getting the result. The match that decides your tournament is rarely the one you're in — it's the one that quietly holds the knife.

📰 Match report · the carnage, the fairy tales, the fallen

The group stage closed and the Round of 32 opened with the kind of fortnight that rewrites cards and ends careers. Here is the football, with one eye on the office ledger it keeps detonating.

💥 The upset that swallowed the headlines — Paraguay knock out Germany

1–1, then penalties, then bedlam. Germany — top-ten, four-time champions — are out in the Round of 32, and the football world reached for superlatives: "potentially the greatest upset in World Cup history" (Al Jazeera), Germany's first-ever World Cup shootout defeat. A German goal chalked off by VAR; Klopp, incandescent on MagentaTV: "If that goal is illegal, then Arsenal won't be English champions." Havertz: "I'm lost for words." 🐐 The fairy tale — Cape Verde, smallest nation ever to reach the knockouts

Population ~525,000, ranked 67th, drawn with Spain and Uruguay — and through. Three group draws (0–0 Spain, a twice-clawed-back 2–2 with Uruguay, 0–0 Saudi Arabia) made Cape Verde "the smallest nation ever to qualify for the World Cup knockout stages." They reward Michael and Divisha — and now stand opposite Messi's Argentina in the last 32.

😬 The fallen — Türkiye, Tunisia, Uruguay

Türkiye, a pre-tournament dark-horse pick, crashed out having taken 62 shots without scoring across their first two games — billed as the most shots without a goal in any two-match span in World Cup history — before a consolation 3–2 over a rotated USA. A grim watch for Mike's double-Turkey. Tunisia were branded "the worst team at the 2026 World Cup" (5–1 Sweden, 4–0 Japan, 3–1 Netherlands), their manager the first in World Cup history sacked after matchday one — Josh's dead double. And Uruguay, the highest-ranked side to fall in the groups, undone by a Muslera howler against Spain — a top Kerrill had already called, having sold Uruguay near its peak for 6.50. Spare a thought for Haiti too: no goals, goal difference of −11, statistically the tournament's worst.

🌟 Who's looking strong

France top the pundits' boards — Dembélé in "Ballon d'Or" form, Mbappé "on a mission" — for Kelvin and Giannis. Mexico were perfect: three wins, not a single goal conceded, the home crowd a "12th man," gilding Divisha's fortress. Argentina rolled through with Messi scoring in a seventh straight World Cup game (Tim, Dea). Brazil — "the most fun Brazil side in a while" — edged Japan via a late Martinelli winner to carry Elaine and CJ onward. And ESPN's "biggest climbers," a Colombia branded a "sleeper candidate," sit doubled-up in Kerrill's hand — the model's third-favourite for the office crown.

😤 …and England, being England

Top of Group L, unbeaten, and watched through gritted teeth. A 0–0 with Ghana drew Paul Merson's "back to reality" and a familiar lament about "labored and grim" football; Tuchel, deadpan: "The football becomes so complicated with the appearance of an opponent." The model could not care less about the aesthetics — Neil's England ×2 are its outright title favourites at 20.6%. Pocket aces don't have to be pretty.
