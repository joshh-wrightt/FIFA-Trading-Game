👑 Long live the champion

It is over, and it is hers. Divisha is the champion of the World Cup 2026 office trading game — 35.29 on the board, more than three clear of the field, and she crossed the line owning not a single team. That is not an accident. It is the whole thesis of how she won.

Go back and watch the shape of her tournament and you see a manager who treated shares like a trader, not a supporter. She banked the points while the going was good, then sold the assets before the results could turn against her. Spain — the eventual world champions — passed through her hands: she sold one share to Rohit for 6.75 back in the Round of 32, taking the cash and the certainty. She sold Mexico to Haydee for 2.90 the morning of the Round of 16, hours before England knocked them out. And in her last and coldest call, she sold her Argentina share to Paulius for 2.15 on the eve of the quarter-finals — handing the tournament's most dangerous ticket to her nearest rival, and pocketing the money. By the whistle she held nothing but 8.29 in cash and 27 points already safely in the bank. Nobody could take a single one of them off her on the pitch, because she no longer owned anything on the pitch. The perfect exit.

This is the sequel the last edition promised. When "The King is Dead" went to press, Divisha had just deposed month-long leader Paulius by 0.95. She spent the knockouts turning that knife-edge into a procession.

🗺️ The road to the final

The bracket that decided it all played out over ten days, and it was brutal at the top.

  • Quarter-finals. France saw off Morocco 2–0; Spain ground down Belgium 2–1; England edged Norway 2–1; and Argentina, roared on by a 39-year-old Messi refusing to leave quietly, dismantled Switzerland 3–1.
  • Semi-finals. Spain were imperious, Lamine Yamal pulling France apart in a 2–0 that flattered the losers. And in the tie that lit the fuse for everything that followed, Argentina beat England 2–1 in a semi-final the neutrals will remember less for the football than the needle — of which, more below.
  • Third place. A ludicrous consolation: England 6, France 4. Ten goals, no defending, everyone already thinking about the flight home.
  • The final. Spain 1, Argentina 0. Yamal's tournament, Yamal's trophy — one goal enough to settle it, and one goal enough, as it turned out, to settle the office title too.

🎲 The last-ditch gamble · Paulius goes all-in on Argentina

If Divisha won this like a trader, Paulius tried to win it back like a gambler — and came within a single goal of pulling it off.

The deposed King was never going to go quietly either. On the morning of 10 July, with the quarter-finals about to begin, he went hunting for the one team that could still win him the tournament in a single stroke: Argentina. He bought both shares that same morning — one from Divisha for 2.15, the other from Tim for 2.15 — cornering the market on Messi's last dance. It was a beautiful piece of aggression. Argentina duly beat Switzerland, then beat England, and every step of that run banked +4 points into Paulius's column and dragged him right back to the shoulder of the leader.

And then the maths got cruel. Paulius finished on 31.29, in 4th. Argentina lost the final 1–0. Had they won it, his two shares would have banked +4 more — lifting him to exactly 35.29, dead level with Divisha's 35.29, and ahead of her on the points tie-break (29 to 27). Spain's only goal of the final was, quite literally, the difference between Paulius lifting the trophy and Paulius finishing fourth. He bet the house on Argentina going all the way. They went all the way to the final. One goal short.

🤬 The disgrace in the final · Argentina lose the plot

Someone should say it plainly: the way the Argentina players behaved in the final was a disgrace to a showpiece occasion. Beaten by the better side and unable to lay a glove on Yamal, they reached for the dark arts they had already trialled against England in the semi — a match a British newspaper catalogued as 31 separate acts of gamesmanship, from Mac Allister's crunching challenge inside the first minute to Paredes shoving Bellingham, Romero grappling to kill counter-attacks, and Simeone swinging an arm at Djed Spence's face and somehow staying on the pitch. In the final they simply turned the dial up. The tackles came in late and high, studs showing, long after the ball had gone; niggle became shoving, shoving became scuffling, and by the closing stages the referee had all but lost control of a fixture that should have been a celebration. When the whistle finally went, the handshakes turned into a full-blown melee — players squaring up, benches emptying, staff wading in to pull people apart, the fighting spilling on well after the trophy had been lifted. A team that could have lost like champions chose instead to lose like this. Spain won the World Cup. Argentina won the sin-bin.

🃏 The champion the market made

One last note for the purists who think the draw decides these things. Strip every trade out and hand everyone back their original four teams, and Divisha would have finished second anyway — her drafted Spain-and-USA hand was always strong. The manager who tops that no-trade "ghost" world is Tim, whose drafted Argentina, held to the final, is a championship hand on its own. That is not a knock on Tim: he actually scored more by trading than his frozen self would have, and finished a clear real 2nd. It's simply the nature of the asset. Every team is a depreciating, dividend-paying share — it pays its points to whoever holds it each match, then it is worth less, because it has one game fewer left to pay. So the winners here are the managers who priced those dividends best: Divisha banked her points then sold the emptying shells for cash, Rohit paid up for Spain's dividends off the bottom of the table, and the fallers — Josh, AJ — simply sold theirs too cheap. Your banked points are safe forever; what moves you is how well you price what's still to come. The full counterfactual is laid out in the companion Ghost Table report.
